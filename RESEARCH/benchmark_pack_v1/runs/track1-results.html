Does telling the model “you are an expert” still help?

We built a tough practice exam for an AI that books appliance-repair appointments — the same kind of tool-using job many service businesses want — and checked what actually improves full success.

Short answer

Adding a job title (“you are a scheduling agent”) or a list of soft skills (“careful listening, clear judgment…”) did not clearly help or hurt on this exam.

What did help

Turning on the model’s thinking mode helped a lot. Without it, the model failed much more often — especially when it had to pick the right time slot under messy conditions.

What we tested (in plain English)

Imagine a customer texts something messy about a broken dishwasher. The AI may look up whether you serve that ZIP, list open times, and book a tech. We scored each attempt as a simple pass or fail: did the whole job match the answer key? Tools, fields, and the exact customer reply we allow — almost-right still fails.

Three prompt versions (the only thing that changed for “role”)

Task only
Detailed instructions for the job. No “you are an agent” line.
Task + role
Same instructions, plus: “You are a scheduling agent for an appliance repair company.”
Task + role + soft skills
Same as above, plus generic skills like careful listening and following procedures. No extra coaching for this specific exam.

Two ways the model ran (the only thing that changed for “thinking”)

Instant
Answer quickly — thinking effort set to off.
Thinking on
Same words in the prompt; the API is allowed a medium amount of internal reasoning first.

Every ticket was tried in all six combinations (three prompt versions × two modes), three times each. That is how we got to 2,160 graded attempts.

What kinds of tickets were on the exam?

We mixed easy and hard jobs so the AI could not coast on simple bookings:

  • Read the message only — pull out name, ZIP, appliance (no tools).
  • Stop early — e.g. ask for ZIP, or refuse a non-booking request.
  • Show available times — but do not book yet.
  • Pick the best time — usually the earliest future slot.
  • Full booking — check service area → times → book (or stop for a good reason).
  • Hard cases — same full job, but with traps (wrong ZIP then correction, caller booking for someone else, look-alike slot IDs, slots that are already in the past, and so on).

What we actually wrote in the prompt

This matters: the “task only” version was already long and strict. Role and soft skills were just a small add-on at the end — not a different playbook.

Task only — no role line

(ends after the shared instructions — nothing about “you are a scheduling agent”)

Task + role — one extra sentence

- You are a scheduling agent for an appliance repair company.

Task + role + soft skills — one longer sentence

- You are a scheduling agent for an appliance repair company with strong professional competencies in careful listening, clear judgment, accurate follow-through, and disciplined adherence to procedures.

Underneath all three, the shared instructions already said things like: use the tools when needed; return one JSON answer; use our exact field names; if Alex calls for Sam, book Sam; never book a time that is not in the future; when several times work, take the earliest; and the customer reply must match one of a short list of allowed phrases word-for-word.

What we found (the useful parts)

Overall success was high — about 9 in 10 attempts passed. The useful story is where the 157 failures happened, and what fixed them.

1. Most failures were “thinking off”

Out of 157 failures, about 4 out of 5 happened when the model ran in instant mode. Thinking mode did not just nudge the score — it removed a large pile of mistakes.

So: if you only tune the personality line and leave thinking off, you are optimizing the wrong knob.

2. Picking the right time slot is where thinking mattered most

On tickets whose whole job was “choose the correct appointment time” (without booking yet), instant mode passed about 84% of the time. With thinking on, that jumped to about 99%.

That is the biggest jump we saw by ticket type. Full booking jobs and hard trap tickets also got clearly better with thinking — but slot-picking was the clearest example.

So: “earliest future slot among look-alike IDs” is a reasoning problem, not a job-title problem.

3. A clear rule beat soft skills — we watched one trap disappear

Early practice runs failed when someone called for another person (“Liam calling for Nora…”). The model booked the caller instead of the customer.

We added one plain rule: book the person the appointment is for, not the person on the phone. On the main exam, every attempt with that trap passed (zero failures on that pattern).

So: specific operating rules beat “be a careful listener.”

4. Some traps still hurt — and thinking helps unevenly

These patterns still caused a noticeable share of failures:

  • Look-alike time-slot IDs (like slot_017 vs slot_071) — the model picks the wrong one by “looks first,” not by earliest clock time.
  • Times that are already “now” or in the past — instant mode still tries to book them sometimes.
  • Odd appliances (ice maker, wine cooler) — it must mark them as “other / unsupported,” not pretend they are a fridge.
  • Empty availability that must be worded a certain way — one ticket failed every single time (see below).

So: thinking mode cleans up a lot of slot/time mistakes; it does not fix every wording fork in the rulebook.

How it failed — real examples

These are easier to understand than codes. Each one is a real miss from the exam.

Wrong time picked among look-alikes

“Quinn at my house in 10012. Dishwasher stops mid-cycle; neighbors keep asking about the water noise.”

The tool returned three times. The earliest was slot_071 at 8:45. Two others looked similar (slot_017, slot_017A) but were later.

We required Book the earliest time (slot_071), even if booking later fails on purpose.
Model did Booked slot_017 — the ID that “looks” like it comes first, not the earliest clock time.

A soft-skills sentence did not fix this. Thinking mode usually did.

Said “we’re busy” when the answer key wanted a different empty-times phrase

“Rita from the office break room at 10003. Refrigerator is warm…”

The times tool returned an empty list. Our answer key wanted a specific status and sentence about “no valid future times.” The model always answered with the “sorry, no times available right now / busy” wording instead.

We required Status = no valid future times · exact phrase about that.
Model did (all 18 tries) Status = busy · different allowed phrase about “no times right now.”

Neither role nor thinking fixed this. The rulebook has two ways to talk about “no times,” and the model always picks the wrong fork for this ticket. That is a product/prompt design issue.

Gave up on a slot that was actually valid

“Ava here, 10001. Refrigerator at my apartment is warm. Neighbor says mornings are quieter…”
We required Pick the valid future slot and say a time is available.
Model did (instant + soft-skills prompt) Stopped as if nothing was free (“busy”).

Classic instant-mode miss: it under-reads the list of times.

What we fixed on purpose: caller vs customer

“Liam calling for Nora at apartment 10001. The washer is banging hard on spin…”
We required Book under Nora, not Liam.
After we wrote that rule clearly Every attempt with this trap passed on the main exam.

Write the operating rule. Do not hope “careful listening” invents it.

In ordinary language — what went wrong most often

When we labeled each failure with a primary reason:

  • ~1 in 3 — skipped a tool it was supposed to call
  • ~1 in 4 — called a tool it should not have called
  • ~1 in 9 — used the wrong customer sentence from our allowed list
  • ~1 in 12 — wrong appliance type (e.g. ice maker treated as a refrigerator)
  • Many failures also had the wrong final status or the wrong chosen time ID as a follow-on mistake

The role result (the official question)

We decided the rule before looking at the big run: role “helped” or “hurt” only if the difference between prompt versions, averaged ticket-by-ticket, was clearly away from zero (our 95% confidence band did not touch zero).

Averaged across tickets, success sat around 92–93% for all three prompt versions — within about one to one-and-a-half points of each other. Under that pre-set rule, none of the role comparisons cleared the bar (with thinking off, with thinking on, or combined).

Looking only at instant mode, soft skills even looked a bit worse on some harder job types (list times / pick a time / full book) — but that gap went away when thinking was on, and it is not part of the official claim. Treat it as a hint: extra soft language does not replace careful tool use when the model is rushing.

What this is not claiming

  • Not “role prompts never help anywhere.”
  • Not a test of soft skills without a role line — we never ran that.
  • Not free-form customer wording — we required exact allowed phrases.
  • One workflow (appliance scheduling), one model family.

What to do with this

If your agent already has clear tools, field names, and booking rules, do not expect a job-title line or a soft-skills paragraph to move full-job success much in a setup like this.

Put the effort where the failures lived:

  • Turn thinking on for jobs that choose times and filter “past vs future.”
  • Write specific rules for the traps you have already seen (caller vs customer, never book the past).
  • Clean up confusing forks in your own answer key (two different “no times” phrases will keep failing forever).

Spend prompt budget on truth about the job — not on sounding like an expert.
